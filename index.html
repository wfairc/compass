How a compass works

A compass needle is a small magnet. The Earth acts like a huge magnet, so the needle lines up with its field and one end points toward magnetic north. The rest of the compass helps you read the needle and walk a direction.

Your phone has no needle. A chip (magnetometer) senses the Earth's field. Magnets, steel, cars and speakers throw it off, just like a needle.

Types of compass

Marine (card). A card with the compass points floats in liquid on a magnet. The ship turns around the card; a fixed line (the lubber line) shows the heading. Try Marine, set a bearing, and steer until the line matches it.

Baseplate (orienteering). A clear plate with a turning ring. Set the bearing you want on the ring, then turn your whole body until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow. The travel arrow now points the way to go. Mirror models let you sight the target and see the needle at once. More accurate.

Lensatic (military). A floating card with a sighting wire and slot. Line the target up in the wire and read the card through the lens, both in one view. A prismatic compass does the same with a prism instead of a lens. Neither corrects for declination, so you do that yourself.

The phone can't copy a mirror or lens. Each view shows the same reading the way that compass would.

Bearings and back bearing

A bearing is a direction in degrees clockwise from north: 0 north, 90 east, 180 south, 270 west. The back bearing is the way you came: add 180 (or subtract 180 if the total passes 360). Walk 70 and the way back is 250.

To use the Bearing control: pick a number, or tap Use heading while pointing at something. Baseplate and Marine then tell you which way to turn, and Lensatic shows the back azimuth.

True north and declination

Magnetic north is not true (geographic) north. The gap is called declination. In Chandler it is about 10° east, so a magnetic needle points about 10° to the right of true north.

Compass to map: add east declination. Map to compass: subtract it. Rule: east, magnetic least. In Chandler, true 90 = magnetic 80.

Navigate with a map and compass

1. Take a bearing from the map. Lay the plate edge from you to the target, travel arrow forward. Turn the ring so its lines match the map's north lines, N at top. Read the bearing at the index line.

2. Correct for declination. A map bearing is a true bearing, but the needle is magnetic. In Chandler (about 10° east), subtract the declination: a true bearing of 90 becomes a magnetic bearing of 80 on the compass. In the app, set True or Magnetic to match what you are comparing against.

3. Follow it. Lift the compass off the map, hold it flat, and turn until red is in the shed. Look along the travel arrow, pick a landmark on that line, and walk to it. Then take the bearing again from there. Short legs beat long ones.

4. Where am I? Sight two or three landmarks you can find on the map. Take each bearing, add 10° (Chandler) to make it true, add or subtract 180 for the back bearing, and draw the lines from each landmark on the map. You are where the lines cross.

5. Around an obstacle. Turn 90° and count paces until clear. Turn back to your bearing and walk past it. Turn 90° the other way, walk the same count, then carry on along your bearing.

Directions without a compass

Sun and a stick. Push a stick upright. Mark the end of its shadow, wait 15 minutes, mark it again. The line from the first mark to the second runs roughly west to east.

Polaris. In the northern hemisphere the North Star is within about 1° of true north. Find it by following the two end stars of the Big Dipper's bowl, or look for the W of Cassiopeia on the opposite side. Its height above the horizon equals your latitude (about 33° in Chandler).

Rough clues. The Sun rises roughly east and sets roughly west. At solar noon it is due south (north of 23.4°N, which includes Arizona), and its shadow points north.

Your answer

Land navigation course

A plain-language summary of the standard methods taught in military land navigation courses, written from general knowledge. It is not the official manual and not a replacement for training or practice on the ground.

1. Reading the map: grids, scale, contours

Grid coordinates. Read right, then up (“walk in the house, then up the stairs”). A 6-digit grid, like 123 456, names a spot to within 100 m. An 8-digit grid, like 1234 5678, is good to 10 m.

Scale. Use the bar scale at the bottom of the map to turn map distance into ground distance. Lay a strip of paper along the route, mark it, and slide it onto the scale.

Contour lines join points of equal height. Lines close together mean steep; far apart means gentle. Every fifth line is a bold index line with a number. A closed ring is a hilltop. A ridge is high ground with lower ground on both sides. A saddle is the dip between two peaks. A draw is a small valley: its contour V’s point uphill. A spur is a finger of high ground: its V’s point downhill.

Margin information. Check the map’s date, scale, contour interval and the declination diagram before you use it.

2. Azimuths and back azimuths

An azimuth is a direction in degrees, clockwise from north, 0 to 360. 90 is east, 180 south, 270 west.

Back azimuth (the way back): add 180 if the azimuth is less than 180, subtract 180 if it is 180 or more. A back azimuth of 70 is 250, and of 250 is 70.

3. Three norths and the grid-magnetic (G-M) angle

True north is the North Pole. Grid north is the direction of the map’s vertical grid lines. Magnetic north is where the needle points. The declination diagram on the map shows how they differ, and the G-M angle is the gap between grid north and magnetic north.

A map azimuth is a grid azimuth, and a compass reads magnetic, so you convert in between. Map to compass: subtract if magnetic is east (right), add if west (left). That is LARS: Left Add, Right Subtract. Compass to map: the reverse. Check the diagram. In this app, True N already adds Chandler’s declination for you. Grid north differs from true north by about half a degree here, so use the map’s G-M angle.

4. Using the lensatic compass

Center-hold: open the compass, thumb through the loop, elbows tucked at your sides, the compass in the middle of your chest. Turn your whole body, not your wrists. This is quick and good for walking an azimuth.

Compass-to-cheek: hold the compass up to your cheek, sight along the wire and the slot at the target, and read the card through the lens. This is slower and more accurate, and it is how you shoot an azimuth to a landmark.

Keep the compass away from vehicles, power lines, steel and weapons. They pull the needle. Rough safe gaps from the Army manual: power lines 55 m, vehicles 18 m, barbed wire 10 m (check before relying on them).

5. Intersection: find a point you can see

Use this to locate something you can see but cannot reach, such as a tower or a smoke plume.

  1. From where you stand, shoot an azimuth to the target and convert it to a grid azimuth.
  2. Plot the line on the map from your known spot.
  3. Move to a second known spot and repeat.
  4. The target is where the lines cross. A third line checks it.
6. Resection: find where you are

Lost? Use two or three landmarks you can find on the map.

  1. Orient the map to north and pick landmarks that are well apart. Lines that cross near 90° are best; shallow crossings are sloppy.
  2. Shoot a magnetic azimuth to each landmark and convert it to grid.
  3. Work out each back azimuth (add or subtract 180).
  4. Plot a line from each landmark along its back azimuth.
  5. You are where the lines cross. With three lines, the small triangle they make shows how good your shots were. A big triangle means recheck.

In this app, the Lensatic style helps: aim, tap Use heading to save the sighted bearing, and read the back azimuth.

7. Modified resection: you are on a road or stream

If you are on a linear feature such as a road, trail or stream, one landmark is enough.

  1. Shoot an azimuth to one landmark you can find on the map.
  2. Convert it and work out the back azimuth.
  3. Plot the back-azimuth line from the landmark.
  4. You are where that line crosses the linear feature.
8. Polar coordinates: describe a place by direction and distance

A polar report gives a start point, a direction and a distance, for example: “from the water tower, azimuth 135, 800 m.” To find it, plot the start point, draw a line along the grid azimuth, and measure off the distance on the map scale.

9. Pace count and dead reckoning

Pace count. Count each time the same foot lands (a double pace). Walk a known 100 m on flat ground and count: most people land around 60 to 70. Your own number is the one that matters.

You take more paces uphill, in sand or mud, at night, with a pack or tired. Fewer downhill. Keep tally with knots in a cord or beads, one per 100 m.

Dead reckoning is navigating by azimuth and distance alone. Break the trip into legs. Each leg is one azimuth and one distance. Walk the azimuth, count the paces, and at the end of each leg stop, check, and start the next.

10. Terrain association and route planning

Terrain association means matching the ground to the map as you go, not just following a number.

  1. Orient the map to north and find where you are.
  2. Plan in legs between checkpoints you can recognize.
  3. Handrails: a long feature running your way, like a ridge, road or stream, that keeps you on track.
  4. Catching features: a feature past your target that says you overshot, such as a road or a river.
  5. Attack point: an easy-to-find feature near a hard-to-find target. Navigate to it, then take a short, careful leg in.
  6. Aiming off: aim deliberately to one side of a linear target so you know which way to turn when you reach it.
  7. Steering marks: pick a far object on your azimuth, walk to it, then pick the next. This is faster than staring at the compass.

Go around obstacles instead of through them. At a block, turn 90° and count paces until clear, walk past on your azimuth, turn 90° back the same count, then resume.

11. Night and bad weather

Go slower and rely on pace count, handrails and catching features more than on seeing landmarks. Use a red light to read the map and compass, which keeps your night vision. Re-check your position more often. The luminous marks on a compass help you hold an azimuth, but they do not replace checking the map.

12. Common mistakes

Using the wrong north (grid versus magnetic) or applying the declination the wrong way. Holding the compass near metal. Not counting paces on slopes. Trusting one shot instead of two or three. Walking on an azimuth for too long without a checkpoint. Reading a grid reference up before right.

Why the reading drifts

Hold the phone flat, away from metal, phone cases with magnets, laptops and cars. If the badge says poor, move the phone in a figure 8 a few times to recalibrate. Tilt matters too: the heading is best when the phone is flat. For altitude angle, hold it upright, back toward the target.
